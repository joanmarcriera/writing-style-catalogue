# Tone Spectrum

Tone is the single variable that most determines whether a document is trusted, ignored, or resented. A technically accurate board paper written in a casual tone will be discounted. A friendly internal update written in legalese will create anxiety. Choose tone deliberately — not by accident, not by habit.

## The spectrum (Very Formal ↔ Casual)

**Very Formal**
Legal documents, formal policy, regulatory submissions, board resolutions
- Language: "Shall", "herein", "aforementioned", "the Board resolved"
- Structure: Numbered sections, defined terms, passive voice acceptable
- Contractions: None
- Example: "The Directors shall procure that no material transaction is entered into without prior written approval of the Board."

**Formal**
Board papers, tax technical notes, external professional communications, regulatory responses
- Language: Clear and measured; no contractions; "we recommend" not "we'd recommend"
- Structure: Organised with numbered sections and clear headings
- Example: "We recommend approval of the capital investment. The projected return of £1.5M annually supports the payback period of 18 months."

**Professional**
Executive memos, business cases, client briefings, consulting reports
- Language: Clear, direct, and accessible; contractions avoided in formal sections; "we" preferred over passive voice
- Structure: Logical, with headers; not rigidly numbered
- Example: "The cloud migration will save £800k per year from 2027. Here is why the Azure option is the right choice."

**Professional-conversational**
Internal executive updates, team briefings, stakeholder emails, project status updates
- Language: Natural and direct; contractions acceptable; "we" and "I" used freely
- Structure: Relaxed but organised; bullets preferred over long paragraphs
- Example: "Revenue is up 8% — ahead of plan. The cloud migration is on track. Here's what we need from you."

**Conversational**
Internal updates, team communications, knowledge-sharing posts, informal briefings
- Language: Natural speech patterns; contractions normal; inclusive and warm
- Structure: Minimal formality; short paragraphs; bullets
- Example: "Great news — we've closed the Meridian deal. Three things to know for next week."

**Casual**
Slack and Teams messages, informal check-ins, internal social posts
- Language: Natural speech; abbreviations; emoji acceptable
- Structure: None required
- Example: "Done! Draft's in the shared folder — take a look when you get a chance 👍"

## Tone by document type

| Document type | Correct tone | Common mistake |
|---------------|--------------|----------------|
| Board paper | Formal | Using "professional" tone — too casual for governance records |
| Tax technical note | Formal | Over-hedging ("it might perhaps be argued") — weakens credibility |
| Executive memo | Professional | Burying the recommendation in formal throat-clearing |
| Business case | Professional | Adopting Very Formal tone — makes the case feel defensive |
| Executive update | Professional-conversational | Writing a report when a brief would do |
| Runbook | Professional, terse | Adding narrative context — users under pressure need imperatives |
| FAQ | Professional-conversational | Answering questions the reader didn't ask |
| SOP | Professional | Using passive voice for steps — "the form should be submitted" vs. "submit the form" |
| Job cover letter | Professional-conversational | Either too formal (stiff) or too casual (flippant) |
| Supplier outreach email | Professional-conversational | Marketing-speak that destroys credibility |
| Staff change communication | Conversational | Corporate tone that breeds distrust |

## Tone mistakes to avoid

**The Hedge Spiral**
Excessive qualification that makes the writer sound uncertain even when they are not.
- Bad: "It might perhaps be worth considering whether it could potentially be helpful to explore..."
- Good: "We recommend exploring this further. Our confidence is moderate — here is why."
- Fix: State your view. Qualify with a confidence level. Do not hedge every sentence.

**The Passive Evasion**
Passive voice used to avoid accountability or sound more formal.
- Bad: "Mistakes were made. The decision was taken to proceed."
- Good: "The team made an error in the cost model. We decided to proceed despite the risk."
- Fix: Use active voice. Name who did what. Passive voice is acceptable for describing processes ("the form is submitted to...") but not for attributing decisions or problems.

**The Filler Paragraph**
Opening paragraphs that say nothing before getting to the point.
- Bad: "In today's rapidly evolving business landscape, organisations are increasingly faced with the challenge of..."
- Good: "Revenue fell 12% in Q3. Here is what happened and what we are doing about it."
- Fix: Delete your opening paragraph. The second paragraph is almost always where the actual content starts.

**The Jargon Screen**
Technical or professional jargon used to signal expertise rather than to communicate.
- Bad: "We need to leverage our synergistic core competencies to optimise value delivery."
- Good: "We need to use what we are already good at to deliver more value to clients."
- Fix: Read the sentence aloud. If it would sound strange in a direct conversation, simplify it.

**The False Formality**
Adopting formal language for documents that do not require it — creating distance and reducing impact.
- Bad: "I write to enquire whether it would be convenient to schedule a meeting at your earliest convenience to discuss the aforementioned proposal."
- Good: "Can we schedule 30 minutes this week to discuss the proposal? I am free Tuesday or Wednesday afternoon."
- Fix: Match your tone to the context, not to your idea of what "professional" sounds like.

## Before/after tone adjustments

### Example 1: Executive update (too formal → professional-conversational)

**Before (too formal)**
"The organisation has achieved revenue growth of 8% against plan for the period under review. Challenges have been encountered in relation to recruitment activities. It is anticipated that the forthcoming quarter will present opportunities for acceleration."

**After (professional-conversational)**
"Revenue is 8% ahead of plan — our strongest Q2 in four years. Hiring is behind schedule (4 of 8 roles unfilled) and we have extended to two new agencies. Next quarter: three enterprise bids in final evaluation; we expect to close at least two."

### Example 2: Tax note conclusion (over-hedged → appropriately confident)

**Before (over-hedged)**
"It might perhaps be considered, on balance, that there is an argument to be made that the earn-out could potentially be treated as capital under TCGA 1992, though reasonable minds might differ, and HMRC may conceivably take a different view."

**After (appropriately confident)**
"It is our view, with moderate confidence, that the earn-out is correctly treated as capital under TCGA 1992 s.48. HMRC may challenge this position if the earn-out is linked to continued employment — we recommend the SPA contains explicit language confirming it is not."

### Example 3: Change communication (corporate → conversational)

**Before (corporate)**
"The organisation is embarking on a transformational journey to modernise its identity and access management capabilities. Colleagues will be required to complete the MFA registration process by 30 June 2025."

**After (conversational)**
"From 1 July, you will need to use two-step login (MFA) to access company systems. This takes 5 minutes to set up. Here is why we are doing it and how to do it before the deadline."

## Tone and audience table

| Audience | Appropriate tone | Key principle |
|----------|-----------------|---------------|
| Board of directors | Formal | Unambiguous; no informality; language is part of governance record |
| C-suite (CEO, CFO, CTO) | Professional | Lead with the conclusion; they will re-ask your question in formal language if needed |
| Senior managers | Professional | Direct; respect their time; do not over-explain |
| Engineering team | Professional, technical | Precise and specific; jargon is acceptable and often clearer |
| Non-technical staff | Conversational | Assume good faith; avoid acronyms without expansion |
| External clients | Professional | Match their register; formal for legal and tax; conversational for account management |
| Regulators | Formal | Precise; no ambiguity; every statement is a legal position |
| General public | Conversational | Plain English; no jargon; no assumptions about prior knowledge |

---

Tone affects credibility and impact. Choose deliberately — every document you write creates an impression of you and your organisation before the reader has evaluated a single fact.
