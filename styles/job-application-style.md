# Job Application Style

## What this style is

A job application in this style is a targeted, specific piece of personal writing that answers a single question: why you, for this role, at this organisation, at this time? It is not a prose version of your CV — it adds what a CV cannot: voice, motivation, and a narrative that connects your experience to this specific opportunity.

The greatest mistake in job applications is genericism. A cover letter that could have been sent to ten other companies tells the hiring manager nothing about whether you have thought about this role specifically. The most effective applications demonstrate genuine research into the organisation — its work, challenges, or purpose — and connect that research to specific examples from the applicant's own history.

**Core principle**: Why you → Why them → What you would bring → What you are asking for. Every element must be specific to this role and this organisation.

## When to use it

- Cover letters for specific roles where the application process includes or accepts a letter
- LinkedIn "About" section rewrite for a career change or first professional role
- Personal statements for postgraduate applications or professional development programmes
- Introductory emails for networking outreach ahead of a formal application
- Career change explanations — framing a non-traditional background as an asset
- Self-introduction emails to a recruiter or hiring manager after a referral

## When not to use it

- Generic mass applications sent without role-specific customisation — this style requires tailoring for each application
- Technical assessments, case studies, or work samples — these are content challenges, not writing style challenges
- Formal business communication — use [BLUF](bluf-bottom-line-up-front.md) or [Consulting Style](consulting-style.md) instead
- Teaching or training materials — use [Tutorial Style](tutorial-style.md) or [Socratic Explainer](socratic-explainer.md) instead

## Core structure

```
[OPENING — WHY THIS ROLE AND THIS ORGANISATION]
Not a generic "I am applying for the role of X."
A specific statement about what drew you to this organisation or this role.
Shows you have done research — names something specific about their work.
2–4 sentences.

[YOUR RELEVANT EXPERIENCE — WITH A SPECIFIC EXAMPLE]
The most relevant thing you have done that connects to this role.
One specific, concrete example: the situation, what you did, the outcome.
Quantify where possible. Show, do not tell.
3–5 sentences.

[WHAT YOU WOULD BRING — CONNECTED TO THEIR NEEDS]
2–3 specific ways your background fits their stated needs.
Reference the job description or something you know about their challenges.
Not a list of adjectives — a demonstration through evidence.
3–4 sentences.

[WHAT EXCITES YOU ABOUT THIS OPPORTUNITY]
One or two specific things about this role or organisation.
Authentic — not what anyone would say, but what you specifically feel.
1–2 sentences.

[CALL TO ACTION]
Direct and confident: "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss this further."
Contact details or confirmation of availability.
No apologetic hedging ("I hope you will consider me").
1–2 sentences.
```

## Copy-paste prompt

```
You are an experienced career coach and professional writer. Your task is to write a cover letter or personal statement from the following brief.

AUDIENCE: [AUDIENCE — e.g. "Hiring manager for a Senior IAM Engineer role at a UK cloud security company", "Recruitment team and hiring panel for a Head of IT Operations role", "Graduate admissions tutor for an MSc in Cyber Security"]
PURPOSE: [PURPOSE — e.g. "Cover letter for a Senior Product Manager role at a B2B SaaS company specialising in identity management", "LinkedIn About section for a cloud architect pivoting from implementation to advisory consulting", "Personal statement for an EMBA programme application"]
CONTEXT: [CONTEXT — e.g. "The applicant is currently a Senior Engineer at a financial services firm and has led two large-scale migrations; they have no direct product management title but have informally driven the product direction for their team's tooling for three years"]
INPUT: [INPUT — paste the job description or programme details, the applicant's relevant experience and background, specific examples they want to include, and anything they know about why this specific organisation or role interests them here]
DESIRED_OUTCOME: [DESIRED_OUTCOME — e.g. "Hiring manager reads this and immediately sees the candidate as a credible, interesting person to speak to — not just another applicant", "The cover letter gets the candidate to interview despite a non-traditional background"]
TONE: [TONE — e.g. "Confident and genuine — not formulaic or modest to a fault", "Professional and warm — personal without being informal", "Research-informed — demonstrates specific knowledge of the organisation"]
LENGTH: [LENGTH — e.g. "Cover letter: 350–420 words, one page maximum", "LinkedIn About: 200–280 words", "Personal statement: 550–650 words"]
FORMAT: [FORMAT — e.g. "Letter format with greeting and sign-off", "First-person narrative paragraphs — no bullet points in the main body"]
CONSTRAINTS: [CONSTRAINTS — e.g. "Do not mention the previous employer by name — the candidate is in a notice period and wants discretion", "Do not use bullet points — the hiring manager has specifically said they prefer prose cover letters", "Must fit on one side of A4 in standard 11pt font"]

INSTRUCTIONS:
1. Open with a specific statement about what drew the applicant to this organisation or this role — not "I am writing to apply for." It must name something specific and show genuine research from [INPUT].
2. Include one specific, concrete example of relevant experience. Use the Situation → Action → Outcome structure. Quantify where possible — if [INPUT] contains metrics, use them.
3. Connect the applicant's experience specifically to 2–3 needs stated in the job description or programme requirements from [INPUT]. Do not list generic strengths.
4. Include one or two genuine statements about what specifically excites the applicant about this opportunity — based on [INPUT], not generic enthusiasm.
5. Close confidently with a specific next step or request.
6. Use British English throughout (colour, organisation, licence, favour, practise, etc.).
7. Preserve all facts, job titles, organisations, and specific details exactly as provided in [INPUT]. Do not invent experience or achievements.
8. Distinguish confirmed experience from what the applicant is inferring or positioning — flag inferences with "(inferred)" if needed.
9. Avoid: "I am a passionate [X]," "I have always been interested in," "I feel I would be a great fit," "I am a hard worker," or any other generic opening or claim.
10. Total length must not exceed [LENGTH].

Output the cover letter or personal statement only. No preamble, no commentary.
```

## Output format

A cover letter is a one-page document formatted as a formal letter or a structured email body. It contains:

- **No header** (if sent as an email body) or **letter header** (applicant name, address, date, hiring manager name and address — if sent as a document)
- **Greeting**: "Dear [Hiring Manager Name]" or "Dear Hiring Team" if name is not known — not "To Whom It May Concern"
- **Body**: 4–5 paragraphs of flowing prose — no bullet points in a formal cover letter; bullet points acceptable in a more conversational application email
- **Sign-off**: "Yours sincerely" (if name is known) or "Yours faithfully" (if name is not known) — British convention
- **Contact details**: Email, phone, LinkedIn URL

A LinkedIn "About" section has no formal structure — it is continuous prose in first person. It begins with a hook (what you do or what drives you) and builds to the present.

A personal statement may use light structure (short paragraph headers acceptable) but should read as a coherent narrative.

## Example

**Cover Letter — Head of Identity and Access Management, Veridian Financial Group**

Dear Ms Chen,

Your recent case study on the IAM transformation at Veridian — published in the CISO Alliance newsletter in March — described exactly the challenge I have spent the last three years solving: how to modernise identity controls in a regulated financial services environment without disrupting the production systems that cannot afford downtime. That combination of regulatory complexity, high operational stakes, and the need to move faster than the incumbents is where I do my best work.

In my current role as Senior Identity Architect at Acme Financial Services, I led the 18-month migration from a legacy on-premises identity platform to Okta across 1,850 users and 47 production systems. The migration completed four days ahead of schedule and resolved two open ISO 27001 audit findings in the process. The element I am most proud of is not the technical delivery — it is that we did it without a single identity-related production incident across the entire migration period. That required careful sequencing, parallel running for four months, and a team that could hold both the technical complexity and the risk management in their heads simultaneously.

What draws me to this role specifically is the combination of scale and ambition. Veridian operates 14 times the user base of my current environment, and the 2026 roadmap — public from your annual technology report — includes the move to a federated identity model across your European subsidiaries. That is a genuinely interesting problem. I have experience with federation at smaller scale (three subsidiaries, two countries) and I would relish the opportunity to work at this level of complexity.

I can offer three things this role needs: deep technical knowledge of modern IAM platforms and protocols; experience designing and delivering large-scale migrations in regulated environments without service disruption; and the ability to communicate identity risk clearly to audit committees and regulators, which I have done quarterly for the past two years.

I would welcome the opportunity to discuss how my experience aligns with your needs. I am available for a call at any time this week or next.

Yours sincerely,
Jordan Okafor
jordan.okafor@email.com | 07700 900000 | linkedin.com/in/jordan-okafor

**LinkedIn "About" section — IAM specialist transitioning to advisory**

I have spent eight years making identity systems work in environments that cannot afford for them to fail.

Most of that time has been in financial services — banks, asset managers, and fintech scale-ups — where a misconfigured access control is not a technical embarrassment but a regulatory and reputational event. I have led two large-scale IAM migrations, managed ISO 27001 certification programmes for identity controls, and built access governance processes from scratch for organisations that had none.

What I have learned is that most identity problems are not technology problems. They are process and communication problems that technology can either solve or make worse, depending on whether the implementation is designed around the people who have to use it.

I am now moving into advisory work — helping technology and security leadership teams make better decisions about identity architecture, platform selection, and governance. I bring deep technical knowledge of Okta, Microsoft Entra, and legacy identity platforms; eight years of financial services regulatory context; and a way of explaining complex identity risk that makes sense to audit committees, not just engineers.

If you are working through an IAM decision or a migration and want to speak to someone who has done it under real pressure — I would be happy to have that conversation.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Opening names something specific about the organisation or role — not a generic opener
- [ ] At least one specific, concrete example of relevant experience with a measurable outcome
- [ ] Experience is connected specifically to the stated requirements of the role
- [ ] "What excites me" is genuine and specific — not generic enthusiasm
- [ ] Closing is confident — not apologetic or over-qualified
- [ ] Length is exactly one page (for a cover letter) — not a word over
- [ ] British English used throughout ("Yours sincerely," "practise," "favour," etc.)
- [ ] No generic phrases: "passionate about," "hard worker," "great fit," "excited to share"
- [ ] Every factual claim comes from [INPUT] — no invented achievements
- [ ] The letter could not have been sent to a different role without significant rewriting

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | Fix |
|---------|----------------|-----|
| Generic opening ("I am writing to apply for the role of…") | Tells the reader nothing; every applicant writes this | Open with a specific statement about what drew you to this organisation |
| Prose CV — restating the CV in paragraph form | Duplicates what the reader already has; adds no value | Add what the CV cannot: voice, motivation, and narrative |
| Vague achievements ("led a major project," "improved processes") | Reader cannot assess the claim | Quantify: "Led an 18-month migration across 1,850 users — completed ahead of schedule" |
| Generic enthusiasm ("I am passionate about security") | Every applicant writes this; it signals nothing | Name what specifically about this organisation or role interests you |
| Too long | Hiring managers do not read past one page | Hard limit of one page; cut ruthlessly |
| Apologetic close ("I hope you will consider me") | Lacks confidence; frames the reader as doing the applicant a favour | "I would welcome the opportunity to discuss this" — confident, not pleading |
