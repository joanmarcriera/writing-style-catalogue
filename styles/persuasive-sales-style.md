# Persuasive Sales Style

## What this style is

Persuasive sales style is writing designed to move a reader from their current state — unaware, unconvinced, or uncommitted — toward a specific action. It is built around the reader's problem, not the seller's product. It demonstrates understanding of the prospect's world before offering a solution, and it provides proof that the solution works before asking for anything.

The best sales writing does not feel like sales writing. It feels like a well-informed peer who has identified a relevant problem and has a credible solution. It earns the right to ask for attention by demonstrating genuine understanding, and it earns the right to ask for action by providing evidence that the solution delivers real results.

**Core principle**: Problem → Solution → Proof → Action. Every element must be in service of the reader's gain, not the seller's product features.

## When to use it

- Cold or warm outbound sales emails to prospects who have not yet engaged
- Follow-up emails to prospects who have shown interest but not yet committed
- Proposal cover letters that must make the case before the detailed proposal is read
- Value proposition documents for sales presentations or RFP responses
- Outreach to new potential partners or suppliers from a business development perspective
- LinkedIn outreach to senior decision-makers
- Investor pitch summaries requiring a compelling first impression

## When not to use it

- Internal communications — use [Consulting Style](consulting-style.md) or [BLUF](bluf-bottom-line-up-front.md) instead
- Technical specifications — use [Technical Documentation](technical-documentation.md) instead
- Formal governance documents — use [Board Paper Style](board-paper-style.md) instead
- Situations where the decision has already been made and execution detail is needed

## Core structure

```
[HOOK / OPENING LINE]
One sentence that earns the next read.
Opens with an insight, a problem, or a result — not an introduction.
Never "My name is…" or "I'm reaching out because…"

[PROBLEM RECOGNITION]
Name the specific pain the reader recognises.
Show you understand their world and the cost of the problem.
2–3 sentences maximum.

[SOLUTION POSITIONING]
How you solve that specific problem.
Not a feature list — the mechanism by which you eliminate the pain.
2–3 sentences.

[PROOF / SOCIAL EVIDENCE]
A specific result, customer story, or metric.
Names a comparable organisation and a concrete outcome if possible.
1–2 sentences. Specific and believable — not vague percentages.

[VALUE STATEMENT]
What the reader gains in their specific terms.
Financial, operational, strategic, or emotional benefit.
1–2 sentences.

[CALL TO ACTION]
One specific, low-friction ask.
A question ("Are you available Tuesday?") not a declaration ("I'd love to chat").
Easy to say yes to — 15 minutes, not "a full discovery call."
```

## Copy-paste prompt

```
You are an expert sales copywriter specialising in B2B technology and professional services. Your task is to write a persuasive outbound email or proposal cover letter from the following brief.

AUDIENCE: [AUDIENCE — e.g. "Chief Information Security Officer at a mid-sized UK financial services firm", "Head of IT Procurement at an NHS trust", "Founder of a Series B SaaS company who manages their own cloud infrastructure"]
PURPOSE: [PURPOSE — e.g. "Cold outreach introducing our managed IAM service", "Follow-up after a trade conference conversation", "Proposal cover letter for an identity security audit engagement"]
CONTEXT: [CONTEXT — e.g. "The prospect attended our webinar last month but has not responded to two follow-up emails; they manage IAM for 2,000 users across a hybrid environment"]
INPUT: [INPUT — paste your product or service description, the customer problem you solve, specific proof points, case studies, and the ask you want to make here]
DESIRED_OUTCOME: [DESIRED_OUTCOME — e.g. "Prospect agrees to a 20-minute discovery call", "Prospect reads the attached proposal", "Prospect replies to confirm interest in the pilot programme"]
TONE: [TONE — e.g. "Conversational and genuine — not salesy", "Confident and peer-to-peer — writing as one professional to another", "Warm but direct — not pushy"]
LENGTH: [LENGTH — e.g. "Under 200 words for a cold email", "400–600 words for a proposal cover letter"]
FORMAT: [FORMAT — e.g. "Short paragraphs; no bullet points in the email body", "Subject line + opening hook + problem + solution + proof + CTA"]
CONSTRAINTS: [CONSTRAINTS — e.g. "Do not mention competitor names", "Do not include pricing in this initial outreach", "The prospect is time-poor — every sentence must earn its place"]

INSTRUCTIONS:
1. Open with a hook that earns the next sentence. This must not be an introduction ("My name is…") or a statement about why you are emailing. It must be an insight, a problem the reader recognises, or a result they want.
2. Name the specific problem in concrete terms. Show you understand the cost of the problem — time, money, risk, or competitive disadvantage.
3. Position your solution as the mechanism that eliminates that specific problem. Do not list features — describe the outcome the solution delivers.
4. Provide one piece of specific proof: a named (or anonymised) comparable customer and a concrete, quantified result. Do not use vague social proof ("many companies," "leading organisations").
5. State the value the reader gains in their terms. What changes for them if they act?
6. End with one specific, low-friction call to action. It should be a question ("Are you free Thursday at 10?") not a declaration.
7. Use British English throughout (colour, organisation, licence, favour, etc.).
8. Preserve all proof points and case study details exactly as provided in [INPUT]. Do not invent metrics or customer references.
9. Flag any claim that is an assumption rather than a confirmed fact with "(assumed)" in parentheses.
10. Total length must not exceed [LENGTH].

Output the email or cover letter only. No preamble, no commentary.
```

## Output format

A persuasive sales email is formatted as a plain-text email body with a compelling subject line. A proposal cover letter is formatted as a one-page document. Both share the following characteristics:

- **Short paragraphs**: 2–3 sentences each; single line breaks between paragraphs for scannability
- **No bullet points in cold emails**: Body text reads as a conversation, not a list
- **One call to action**: Never two — multiple asks create indecision
- **Specific and concrete throughout**: Numbers, names, and outcomes rather than generalities
- **No marketing language**: "Revolutionary," "cutting-edge," "best-in-class," "transformative" undermine credibility
- **Subject line** (for emails): Direct and curiosity-inducing — states a benefit or a question; never "Following up" or "Quick question"

Cold email: 150–200 words. Follow-up email: 100–150 words. Proposal cover letter: 350–600 words.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] Opening hook earns the next sentence without using the word "I" in sentence one
- [ ] Problem is named specifically and concretely — not generically
- [ ] Solution is described in terms of outcome, not features
- [ ] Proof point is specific: a named or anonymised comparable customer and a concrete quantified result
- [ ] Value is stated from the reader's perspective — what they gain
- [ ] One specific, low-friction call to action — a question, not a declaration
- [ ] No marketing language ("revolutionary," "best-in-class," "cutting-edge")
- [ ] Total length is within the stated limit for the format
- [ ] British English used throughout
- [ ] All proof points and case study details are from [INPUT] — none invented

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | Fix |
|---------|----------------|-----|
| Opening with "My name is" or "I'm reaching out" | Reader stops reading immediately | Open with an insight or a problem they recognise |
| Feature list instead of outcome | Reader does not care about features; they care about their problem | Describe what changes for them, not how your product works |
| Vague social proof ("many companies," "leading organisations") | Unconvincing; sounds like marketing | Name a specific comparable customer and a specific result |
| Multiple calls to action | Creates indecision; reader does nothing | One ask only — make it easy to say yes |
| Salesy tone or marketing language | Undermines trust; reader disengages | Write as one professional to another; be genuine and specific |
| Too long | Prospect stops reading before the ask | Cold emails: under 200 words; cover letters: under 600 words |
| Subject line "Following up" or "Quick question" | Gets ignored or deleted | Subject line must create genuine curiosity or state a clear benefit |
