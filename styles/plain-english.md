# Plain English

## What this style is

Plain English is a writing discipline, not a style in the sense of formality or tone. It strips away jargon, passive voice, long sentences, and unnecessary complexity to leave writing that any reader can understand immediately — regardless of their background or expertise. It prioritises the reader's comprehension over the writer's desire to sound impressive.

The plain English movement emerged in the 1970s as a reaction to impenetrable government and legal writing. Today, it is the default expectation in most professional communication outside specialist legal, technical, and academic contexts. Plain English does not mean dumbing down — it means being precise enough that the right meaning reaches the right person without friction.

**Core principle**: Clarity over cleverness. Use the simplest word that conveys the exact meaning accurately.

## When to use it

- Change announcements and policy updates for a broad employee audience
- Customer-facing communications including emails, help content, and notifications
- Instructions and how-to guides for mixed-ability audiences
- Summaries of complex documents (legal, technical, or financial) for non-specialists
- Accessibility content for users with lower literacy levels or English as a second language
- Internal guidance documents replacing existing jargon-heavy versions
- Any document where the writer's instinct is to use long words to sound credible

## When not to use it

- Formal legal documents where defined terms and precise modal verbs are required — use [Legal Professional Style](legal-professional-style.md) instead
- Technical reference documentation for expert practitioners — use [Technical Documentation](technical-documentation.md) instead
- Academic writing assessed against scholarly conventions — use [Academic Style](academic-style.md) instead
- Persuasive documents requiring structured logical argument — use [Minto Pyramid Principle](minto-pyramid-principle.md) instead

## Core structure

Plain English has no single structure — it is a set of principles applied to any document type. The structure should match the document's purpose (instructions use numbered steps; announcements use short paragraphs; policies use headed sections). The following template shows the principles in action for a common use case — a policy update:

```
[HEADING]
What this document is about. Short and specific.

[WHY THIS MATTERS — 1–2 SENTENCES]
Why the reader should care. What changes for them.

[WHAT YOU NEED TO KNOW — SHORT PARAGRAPHS OR BULLETS]
The key facts. One idea per paragraph or bullet.
Use "you" and "we" throughout.
Active voice: "We changed X" not "X has been changed."

[WHAT YOU NEED TO DO — NUMBERED LIST IF ACTION REQUIRED]
Specific steps in plain language.
Each step is one action, one sentence.

[WHERE TO GET HELP — BRIEF]
Contact, link, or next step.
```

## Copy-paste prompt

```
You are a plain English writing expert. Your task is to rewrite or create the following content in plain English.

AUDIENCE: [AUDIENCE — e.g. "All staff including non-technical roles", "Customers receiving a service change notification", "New starters in their first week"]
PURPOSE: [PURPOSE — e.g. "Explain the new hybrid working policy", "Notify customers of a price change", "Rewrite a jargon-heavy internal guidance document"]
CONTEXT: [CONTEXT — e.g. "This replaces a six-page policy document that staff consistently say they cannot understand"]
INPUT: [INPUT — paste the original text, notes, or draft here]
DESIRED_OUTCOME: [DESIRED_OUTCOME — e.g. "Any employee can read this and immediately know what has changed and what they need to do"]
TONE: [TONE — e.g. "Warm and clear", "Professional but friendly", "Neutral and factual"]
LENGTH: [LENGTH — e.g. "No more than 400 words", "Same length as original but clearer"]
FORMAT: [FORMAT — e.g. "Short paragraphs with bullet points", "Numbered steps for action items", "Email format with subject line"]
CONSTRAINTS: [CONSTRAINTS — e.g. "Must retain all legal obligations exactly as stated", "Do not change any figures or dates", "Suitable for printing as a single A4 page"]

INSTRUCTIONS:
1. Replace every piece of jargon with the simplest word that carries the same meaning. Use "use" not "utilise"; "start" not "commence"; "help" not "facilitate"; "show" not "demonstrate."
2. Use active voice throughout. Change "X was decided by the committee" to "The committee decided X."
3. Keep sentences under 20 words. Split any sentence over 20 words into two.
4. Write in short paragraphs — three to four sentences maximum. Leave white space between paragraphs.
5. Use "you" to address the reader and "we" for the organisation where appropriate.
6. Use bullet points or numbered lists for anything with more than two items.
7. Spell out all acronyms and abbreviations on first use. Remove those that are not essential.
8. Use British English throughout (colour, organisation, licence, favour, practise, etc.).
9. Preserve all factual claims exactly as provided in [INPUT]. Do not change dates, figures, names, or obligations.
10. Flag any statement in [INPUT] that appears to be an assumption rather than a confirmed fact with "(assumed)."
11. After rewriting, check: Is there any word a 14-year-old would not know? Is there any sentence over 20 words? Is there any passive voice? Fix any that remain.

Output only the plain English version. No preamble, no commentary.
```

## Output format

Plain English does not produce a fixed document format — the format follows the purpose. However, the output will always share these characteristics:

- **Short sentences**: Average 12–18 words; no sentence over 25 words
- **Short paragraphs**: 2–4 sentences; blank line between paragraphs
- **Active voice**: Subject performs the action in every sentence
- **Second person**: "You will receive" not "Employees will receive"
- **Bullet points**: Used for any list of three or more items
- **Numbered steps**: Used for any sequence of actions
- **Headings**: Break content every 150–200 words
- **No jargon**: Every specialist term is explained or replaced

## Example

**Before (jargon-heavy policy extract):**
"The implementation of the aforementioned remote working utilisation protocols necessitates comprehensive stakeholder engagement and iterative refinement mechanisms to ensure that the operationalisation of the policy framework is commensurate with organisational requirements and aligned to the strategic human capital objectives."

**After (plain English):**
"We need your input to make the new remote working policy work. Over the next four weeks, we will hold team sessions to hear what is and is not working. We will use your feedback to improve the policy before it comes into full effect in September."

---

**Plain English policy summary — Privileged Access Management (IAM)**

From 1 June, you will need to request temporary access for any admin-level task on our production systems. Access will last four hours and close automatically.

**What this means for you:**

If you are an engineer, database administrator, or IT support analyst, you will use the new access request form in the IT portal to get temporary admin rights. Your manager approves requests under two minutes; emergency access (P1 incidents) is granted in under 60 seconds via a separate fast-track route.

**Why we are doing this:**

Permanent admin access was involved in two security incidents earlier this year. Temporary access limits the damage if credentials are ever compromised. It also creates a full audit trail for our ISO 27001 compliance obligations.

**What you need to do now:**

1. Log into the IT portal and set up your profile before 25 May.
2. Attend the 30-minute drop-in session (see calendar invite) if you have questions.
3. Contact the IT helpdesk if you cannot access the portal.

Any questions? Email it-security@company.com or speak to your line manager.

## Quality checklist

- [ ] No word over three syllables unless it is the most precise word and is explained on first use
- [ ] All sentences are under 20 words
- [ ] Active voice used throughout — no passive voice
- [ ] No jargon, initialisms, or abbreviations without explanation
- [ ] Paragraphs are three to four sentences maximum
- [ ] Headings break content every 150–200 words
- [ ] Lists use bullet points or numbered steps — not prose
- [ ] "You" and "we" are used to address the reader and the organisation
- [ ] British English used throughout
- [ ] A 14-year-old could read and understand this without assistance

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it's wrong | Fix |
|---------|----------------|-----|
| Replacing jargon with different jargon | Reader still cannot understand | Ask "would my neighbour understand this?" and replace if not |
| Long sentences with multiple clauses | Hard to parse; reader has to re-read | Break at 20 words; one idea per sentence |
| Passive voice | Unclear who did what; sounds evasive | Use active: "We changed the policy" not "The policy has been changed" |
| Dense paragraphs with no white space | Looks hard to read before reading begins | Keep paragraphs to 3–4 sentences; add blank lines between |
| Unnecessary words and qualifiers | Wastes reader time | Cut "in order to," "at this point in time," "due to the fact that" |
| Acronyms without explanation | Excludes readers who do not know them | Spell out on first use: "Identity and Access Management (IAM)" |
| Formal third person | Creates distance; sounds bureaucratic | Use "you" and "we" throughout |
